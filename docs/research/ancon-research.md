# El Ancón de Loíza: Historical Research Dossier

Compiled 2026-09-26. Built for a 3D reconstruction of the Río Grande de Loíza river-ferry crossing across its history.

**How to read this document**
- Each factual claim has a source URL. Short IDs like [S1] point to the source list at the end.
- Confidence tags: **(H)** high, several sources agree or there is a primary or authoritative source. **(M)** medium, one credible source, or a figure measured from modern data. **(L)** low, a single informal source or a weak inference.
- **[INFERRED]** marks my own reasoning or general regional knowledge, not a source that is specifically about the ancón. Treat these as plausible defaults for art direction, not as facts.

## 0. Key corrections to the brief

1. **Piñones is in Loíza, not in Carolina.** Piñones, Vacía Talega and the west landing of the ancón all lie in **barrio Torrecilla Baja, municipality of Loíza**. The Carolina/Loíza line is at Boca de Cangrejos, farther west. The ancón crossed between **Torrecilla Baja (west bank) and Loíza barrio-pueblo, also called Loíza Aldea (east bank)**. (H) [S3][S12][S26]
2. **Bridge.** The replacement is the **PR-187 bridge over the Río Grande de Loíza**. Its official name is **"Puente de la Restauración"**, and it is tagged in OSM as "Puente Río Grande de Loíza". The name refers to the 1970 "restoration" of Loíza Aldea as the municipal seat. (H) [S1][S4][S27]. One opinion column calls it "Puente de los Restauradores" (L) [S20].
3. **Dates.** The bridge was **inaugurated in 1985** (Archivo Negro, El Adoquín). **Regular ancón service ended in 1986** (El Nuevo Día, Mellon, Miyamoto, Archivo Negro). The family then ran a tourist boat, the *Paseadora*, from the old station until about **1987**. (H for 1985/1986; M for 1987) [S1][S3][S4][S5][S6][S7]
4. **Propulsion.** The vessel was **human-powered to the end**. It started with poles and later ran on two taut ropes hauled by hand. Chains are also mentioned. I found **no evidence of a motor** on the working ferry. One AI summary claimed a "mechanized" final era, but the actual El Nuevo Día text says nothing of the kind, so I discarded that claim. (M-H) [S1][S3][S5][S8]
5. **The crossing is far older than 1920.** The Cortijo family took over in 1920. Before that the Iturregui sugar family ran it. Colonial maps and documents show a crossing near the mouth from at least the 1770s–1790s, and an official *ancón de pasaje* was ordered in 1824. (H) [S1][S3]

- The crossing was about **200–230 m** long, corner to corner, measured from OSM/NOAA shoreline data. Width perpendicular to the flow is about **150 m** at the town landing and about **170–190 m** at the bridge line. (M, modern shoreline) [S26]
- The crossing sits roughly **500–600 m upstream of the mouth**. The river runs about SW→NE through its last kilometre, so the ferry moved roughly NW↔SE across it. (M) [S26]
- The bridge was built **immediately upstream / next to the ancón station**. Period photos show the new bridge "in the background" of the station, and a house beside the landing was demolished to build it. (H) [S4]
- **Historical width:** colonial sources call the river *caudaloso* (full-flowing), and its flow was "much greater than today's". Flow dropped after Carraízo Dam was completed upstream (1953–54). (M) [S3][S15]. [INFERRED] Before 1953 the crossing was probably a bit wider and faster, especially in the rainy season.
- Torrech also claims Loíza's settlement "was closer to the mouth" and that "it was not until 1910 that the village moved". **This is probably a misreading.** In January 1910 the *municipal seat* moved to Canóvanas, and the old town became "Loíza Aldea". The Aldea itself did not move. The San Patricio church (1645/1729) still stands where it always has. (M) [S3][S16][S14]

### 1.2 River, mouth, sandbar, estuary
- The Río Grande de Loíza is Puerto Rico's largest river by discharge. It is about 64 km long, drains 751 km², and forms a delta in the Canóvanas/Loíza lowlands. (H) [S13]
- **The mouth closes periodically behind a sand bar.** DRNA calls this "common" and says rain reopens it. In June 2026 manatees were trapped behind a closed mouth, and DRNA cut a channel about 8 ft wide × 4 ft deep. There was a similar manatee rescue in 1995. (H) [S17][S18]
- A 2026 Commons photo of the old ferry site shows a **pale sand spit or dune line with low green scrub** across the water, meaning the bar or spit sits at the mouth just downstream. (H, visual) [S19]
- **Depth at the crossing:** no published figure found. [INFERRED] Poles worked in the early period, so the water was probably about 1.5–3 m deep. The later steel barge with 6–8 cars needed about 0.5–0.8 m draft. (L)
- **Current:** normally slow, but floods and *marejadas* (swells) were strong. Archive photos show floods in Loíza town and at the station, including during the 1980s. (H for flooding) [S4]. The helmsman with the steering pole "had to understand where to place it to move with the river's flow". (H) [S1]
- **Tides:** microtidal. At NOAA San Juan (9755371, epoch 1983–2001) the great diurnal range (MHHW–MLLW) is **0.48 m** and the mean range is **0.34 m**. (H) [S21]
- **Estuary / lagoons:** Piñones lagoon and Torrecilla lagoon lie west along the Piñones coast, separate from the river mouth. Together they hold about 38 species of fish and crustaceans. Piñones lagoon is bioluminescent in some seasons. (H) [S22]
- **Elevation:** Loíza barrio-pueblo sits at about 3 m (10 ft). The municipality is flat coastal plain under 100 m. (H) [S16][S23]

### 1.3 Weather and light
- Average temperature is **25.8 °C**. The dry season runs January–March. (H) [S22]
- Rainfall on the north coast near San Juan is about 1,500 mm a year. The wetter season is roughly April–November. (M) [S24]
- **Trade winds** blow E–NE all year and are especially breezy on the NE coast, so expect ripples on the river and moving palm fronds. (H) [S24]

## 2. The vessel(s)

### 2.1 Names and terms
- **Ancón / barcaza / plataforma.** Operators were *anconeros*, and María Luisa Cortijo is described as the family's only *anconera*. The route was called "El Pasaje". The site was "la estación de El Ancón". (H) [S1][S4][S11]

### 2.2 Construction and size by phase
| Phase | Hull / deck | Capacity | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1770s–1840s (colonial *ancón de pasaje*) | Barge; the Plée drawing (1821–23) shows it with a pole or pike; the Lombera plan (1840s) shows a rope from the right side of the craft to the shore | People, carts, animals, crops (inferred from function) | [S3] (H for existence; L for details) |
| c.1900 | A "photo of the Ancón from 1900" is published in Sepúlveda Rivera, *Puerto Rico Urbano*; image seen in El Adoquín | – | [S3] (M) |
| 1920s–1930s | **Wooden** platform ("El Ancón de madera"), **one vehicle** capacity; poled with **mangrove poles** | 1 car or ox cart plus people and horses | [S4] caption 1 (H) |
| 1930s–1970s | Platform progressively enlarged "for two vehicles, for four, for six" | 2 → 4 → 6 vehicles | [S1] (H) |
| **1980–1986 (final)** | **"Metal barge with steel plates"**, capacity **6–8 vehicles** (ENDI: 8). Hauled out by crane for maintenance about 1982 because snails and other growth fouled the hull | 6–8 cars plus pedestrians and bicycles | [S1][S4] (H) |
| 1987 | ***La Paseadora***: tourist boat run by the family after closure; weekends; $2 adults / $1.50 children | Passengers | [S4][S7] (H) |
| (auxiliary) | A **yola** (small rowboat) run by El Ancón staff to carry people across | Few people | [S4] caption 29 (H) |

**[INFERRED] dimensions for modeling (L):**
- 1930s wooden, one car: about 7–8 m × 3–3.5 m deck of heavy plank on timber stringers, with low sides or open edges and ramp/apron boards at each end.
- 1980s steel, 8 cars (2 lanes × 4): about 20–22 m × 7–8 m. It would be a flat pontoon with welded plate hull, hinged or loose end ramps, a low perimeter rail or curb, and rope guides or rollers on the upstream edge. The Archivo Negro photos show a flat, open deck with cars parked closely and people standing among them.

### 2.3 Propulsion
- **Poles (pértigas/varas).** Early on the platform moved "with a pole of majagüilla or mangrove". There were **two poles: one pushed, the other kept the course**, acting as a rudder. (H) [S1]. A 1930s photo shows Pedro Cortijo "propelling the wooden Ancón with mangrove poles". (H) [S4]
- **Ropes.** "When cars arrived, ropes began to be used: **two marine ropes kept taut between one shore and the other**… **two or three people hauled** and rotated positions according to passenger flow that day." (H) [S1]. A 1930s photo shows Chano Cortijo "propelling El Ancón with ropes". (H) [S4]
- **Chains.** Torrech describes the ancón as "moved with *picas, cadenas y sogas*" (pikes, chains and ropes). (M) [S3]
- **Rope or wire.** A 1982 photographer says it "was pulled by hand across the lagoon by a rope or wire". (M) [S2]
- Mellon calls it a "human-powered barge". (H) [S5]
- [INFERRED] The likely final setup was two parallel hawsers (or wire) anchored on posts or deadmen on each bank, lying across the deck or through fairleads. Crew pulled hand over hand, walking the deck, possibly with wooden hand-grips or "clubs" as on other rope ferries. The ropes would sag into the water between crossings so boats could pass.

### 2.4 Operators ("anconeros")
- **Iturregui family.** Sugar-estate owners whose cane land reached Carolina. They ran the crossing to move cane workers coming from Río Grande, Luquillo and Fajardo in the east and from Carolina and San Juan in the west. It was possibly established in the late 1800s. (H that they ran it; L on the date) [S1]
- **Pedro Cortijo Calderón** ("Papá Pedro") worked the barge for the Iturreguis and **bought it in 1920**, becoming the first *concesionario*. He also bought the adjacent riverside land from the Iturreguis in the 1920s. (H) [S1][S6][S11]
- **Feliciano "Chano" Cortijo** (son) operated it from the 1930s to the 1970s. **Amelia "Tanén" Rosario**, his wife, ran the house, the business and the kitchen (burén cooking), and practised herbal healing. **Andrea "Andreíta"**, Pedro's daughter and a teacher, knew the operation of "El Pasaje". (H) [S4][S11]
- **María Luisa "Magui" Cortijo Rosario** took charge in **1978** after Chano's death. The male operators went on **strike** against her, and she ran the ancón alone. She is the only woman to operate it. She ran it until the 1986 closure. (H) [S4][S6][S11]

### 2.5 Fares, hours, facilities
- **Fares:** **10¢** in the 1920s (per person including the "mode of transport"), later **25¢**, and **$2.50** per vehicle at the end. (H) [S1][S5]
- **Hours:** no source found. [INFERRED] Daylight service, extended for demand. ENDI and Archivo Negro describe heavy weekend family traffic. (L)
- **Landing / station:** "La estación de El Ancón" stood on the Loíza bank next to the Cortijo house, which is now the Casa Museo Cortijo. The house's floor slabs show successive expansions; it was rebuilt or constructed in concrete in the 1960s and painted in its 1980s colours. (H) [S4][S6]
- **Bar Restaurante El Ancón:** the family's bar and dance spot, with a **terrace overlooking the river**. It served typical Loíza food first cooked by Tanén, had a **jukebox (vellonera)**, sold beer and **alcapurrias**, and had **plena musicians on Sundays** in the 1980s. It also ran "the first pizzeria in the town centre". (H) [S1][S4]
- [INFERRED] Signage probably included a hand-painted "EL ANCÓN" on the bar and a fare board. The 1982 Flickr photographer notes "a bar with the same name". (M for the bar name) [S2]

## 4. Did the ancón survive?
- **Regular ferry service ended in 1986**, after the 1985 bridge opening. (H) [S1][S4]
- In 1987 the *Paseadora* tourist boat ran on weekends from the old station. The family's attempt to keep a barge as a tourist attraction did not get municipal support, and **a hurricane swept the barge out to sea**. (H) [S4][S6]
- **Today:** the Colectivo El Ancón de Loíza and the Casa Museo Cortijo occupy the original site at Calle Carlos Escobar #13. A Mellon-funded plan calls for a **new human-powered barge**, but it is not yet documented as built. (H) [S5][S6][S9]
- A June 2026 Commons photo titled "Old Loiza River ferry" shows, at the old landing, a **small moored blue-and-red platform** carrying a car body, strung with festoon lights on two posts, plus a striped black-and-white marker pole. This looks like a commemorative or installation piece, not a working ferry (M, visual interpretation) [S19]. A companion photo shows the view across: **dense green bank vegetation** (mangrove / sea-grape-type shrubs, Casuarina and coconut palms on the skyline) and stakes in the water. [S19b]

## 7. Human and cultural details

- **Afro-Puerto Rican identity.** Loíza is "La Capital de la Tradición". About 64% of residents identify as of African descent. It is the heartland of **bomba** and a cradle of **plena**. Groups include the Hermanos Ayala and the Mayombe group, which Gladys Rivera led in the 1980s. (H) [S12][S23][S4]
- **Fiestas de Santiago Apóstol (late July, about Jul 23–25).** Characters are **vejigantes** (Moors, with coconut-husk masks carrying 3–5 horns and bold paint), **caballeros** (Santiago, wearing wire-mesh masks), **viejos** (in rags with cardboard masks) and **locas** (men dressed as women). Photos show the patron-saint committee in the plaza and youths in costume about 1981. (H) [S36][S11][S4]
- **Church:** Parroquia del Espíritu Santo y San Patricio, 1645, enlarged 1729. Single barrel-vaulted nave, massive walls and buttresses, two-storey three-bay façade, belfry with two bells in a "provincial baroque" style. Described as the island's only fortified-type church. NRHP 1976. (H) [S14]
- **Kiosks and food.** The classic outing was to cross on El Ancón and then do the **chinchorreo** along Loíza's kiosks: jueyes at Richard's, and **Las Carreras** kiosks with food cooked **al burén**. The Piñones kiosk strip dates from the mid-20th century. Typical dishes are alcapurrias, bacalaítos, piononos and coco frío. (H) [S1]; (L) [S31]
- **Weekend family trips.** Families brought *calderos* of food, crossed, spent the day under almond trees and crossed back in the afternoon. One woman remembered driving from Mayagüez via La Piquiña: "it was like going to Disney World". (H) [S1]
- **Celebrities.** Iris Chacón, Tony Croatto, Wilkins, Lucecita Benítez, Cheo Feliciano and the group Los Chicos visited. Commercials, TV specials and films were shot at the ancón. (H) [S1][S4]
- **Julia de Burgos.** Her poem "Río Grande de Loíza" is part of the site's identity. School groups threw flowers into the river on her birthday (Feb 17) in the 1980s. (H) [S1][S4]
- **Before the bridge the town was self-sufficient:** two pharmacies, two cinemas (in town and in Medianía), clothing and shoe stores, colmados, butcher shops and furniture stores. (H) [S1]
- **Vehicles.** Ox carts and horses (1920s), first cars (1930s–40s), **públicos** (shared taxis) by 1959, and bicycles (1980s photos). In the 1970s TV production vans crossed two at a time. (H) [S1][S4]. [INFERRED] Period-typical models: Ford Model T/A (1920s–30s); 1940s–50s American sedans and pickups; 1960s–70s large US sedans and station wagons used as públicos; late 1970s–80s Japanese compacts (Toyota Corolla, Datsun) alongside US cars. (L)
- **Houses.** The Cortijo house was built in concrete in the 1960s, with visible expansions. (H) [S4]. [INFERRED] In 1900–1950 the typical Loíza housing was wooden *casas* on short posts (zocos), roofed in zinc/corrugated iron or earlier thatch (*yagua/paja*), with shutters (*tormenteras*) and bright paint. From the 1960s concrete houses gradually replaced them. (L-M, general PR vernacular)
- **Clothing.** [INFERRED, general PR, L]
  - 1900s–1930s: men in white or light cotton shirts and trousers, straw hats (the *pava* is more of a highland jíbaro style; coastal workers wore straw brims); women in long cotton dresses with head wraps.
  - 1940s–50s: guayaberas and fedoras; women in knee-length print dresses.
  - 1970s–80s: flared jeans, printed shirts, Afro hairstyles, sneakers and baseball caps.
  - 1981 Santiago photos show "non-traditional costumes".

## 10. Open questions / gaps
- Exact official inauguration date of the bridge, its designer and its length. OSM gives about 400 m including approaches; the main span is unknown. An AGPR/ACT file probably exists (see V9).
- Exact hull dimensions, and whether the final rope rig used wire, chain or sheaves.
- Which hurricane carried off the barge. Hugo (1989) is inferred, not confirmed.
- Paving date of the Piñones section of PR-187 and construction date of the Boca de Cangrejos bridge.
- Original images of the Plée drawing (Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle, Paris) and the Lombera plan (probably AGPR or Archivo General de Indias / Servicio Geográfico del Ejército).
